## What a public test lets you check

The Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence is part of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). Consider one report its researchers published. In September, it assessed the cybersecurity capabilities of Z.ai’s GLM-5.3. In one exercise, it gave models browser-engine code with a known flaw, pointed them toward particular files and asked them to trigger the bug. GLM-5.3 succeeded on 74 of 183 tasks. A reader can look up what the testers supplied, what counted as success and how much uncertainty surrounds the score. Finding a bug with those clues is a different task from discovering one unaided. [NIST’s results and methods](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/09/caisis-assessment-zais-glm-53-cyber-capabilities)

In October, the center also published guidance on preparing for incidents during AI training and evaluation. Those two documents answer different questions. September’s assessment tells you what a particular model did in a test. October’s guidance tells a lab how to prepare for something going wrong. Neither tells you how the latest version of ChatGPT performed in a federal evaluation. [NIST’s October guidance](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/10/key-practices-incident-readiness-and-response-super-intelligence-training)

NIST describes the center’s work as testing under voluntary agreements, with a focus on national security risks such as cyberattacks and biological threats. When a company says its model underwent government testing, look for the model version, the tasks and the published findings. If the results are private, the fact you can establish is that the company says testing took place. Whether the evidence supports its safety claim remains an open question. [The center’s remit and published work](https://www.nist.gov/caissi)
