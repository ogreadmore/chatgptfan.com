An AI conversation can feel private and personal. The business holding it can still run out of money. Character.AI's bankruptcy sale puts those two realities together: what protection should follow a conversation when the company changes hands?

In a [November 24 announcement](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-opposes-asset-sale-characterai-amid-child-safety-concerns), California Attorney General Rob Bonta described a multistate objection to the proposed sale. The states say the package includes chat histories associated with more than 21 million U.S. accounts, including over 2.1 million registered to minors. They argue that the proposed terms would not adequately preserve the privacy promises under which that information was collected.

The states want the court to reject the proposal or impose protections, including excluding sensitive information and children's data. These are the states' arguments and requested remedies. An objection is not a finding that the company has broken the law, and it is not an order stopping the sale.

## The bidding process and the privacy question

The procedural details are moving. [PPC Land's November 26 reporting](https://ppc.land/character-ai-asks-judge-to-make-10m-bid-for-users-chats-the-one-to-beat/) describes a November 25 request to designate Smirks and Pangs as the opening bidder, with a $10 million offer. It also reports that the company's request to keep the financial sponsor confidential had been withdrawn. Readers should therefore be careful with earlier descriptions of a still-secret backer.

The same report distinguishes designation of an opening bidder from approval of the sale itself. We have not independently verified a final sale order. The question of what happens to users' conversations should not disappear behind a headline about a bid advancing.

Character.AI's [October announcement and FAQ](https://blog.character.ai/an-update-on-character-ai/) said the process was intended to find a buyer that could keep the service operating. It said existing privacy and security practices remained unchanged and pointed users to account deletion and data export. That is the company's earlier explanation, not a new response to the states' objection.

## What users can check now

Start with the service's own account controls and notices. Decide which conversations you want to retain, inspect export and deletion options, and keep track of changes to the company and its data practices. Do not assume that pressing a button answers every question about retained copies or a proposed transfer. Those details need clear, specific answers from the service.

**Our view:** privacy commitments should remain meaningful when a business is under financial pressure. People who confided in a chatbot should receive understandable notice, usable choices and enforceable protections. Continuing a useful service is a legitimate goal; the people represented in its records also deserve a voice in what happens next.
