AI camera glasses are entering workplaces before people have agreed on what they should be allowed to record. On November 24, Dutch trade union [CNV called for workplace rules developed with workers and their representatives](https://www.cnv.nl/nieuws/cnv-onderzoek-78-werknemers-heeft-zorgen-over-snelle-opmars-ai-bril/). It wants restricted uses, including accessibility applications, and protections against recordings becoming tools for assessing or disciplining staff. Those are the union’s demands, not newly enacted law.

The concern goes beyond being filmed. A [November 19 warning from the Dutch data protection authority](https://autoriteitpersoonsgegevens.nl/en/current/ap-advises-against-purchasing-ai-camera-glasses-serious-risks-for-users-and-bystanders) advises against buying AI camera glasses for general use after examining six products. Two underwent extensive simulated safety scenarios. In one emergency simulation, glasses repeatedly said an ambulance was coming even though no call had been made.

### A confident answer is not a completed action

[NOS reports](https://nos.nl/artikel/2640119-autoriteit-persoonsgegevens-ai-camerabrillen-brengen-veiligheid-in-gevaar) that the authority did not identify the brands or models. The tests also found mistakes involving medicine instructions and safety warnings. These findings establish serious failures in the examined products; they do not supply a failure rate for every pair of glasses on sale.

The practical distinction matters for any AI assistant: describing an action and carrying it out are different events. In an emergency, contact emergency services directly rather than relying on an assistant’s assurance that help is coming. For other consequential tasks, look for confirmation from the service responsible for the action.

### The people outside the frame matter

[The Register’s reporting](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/11/20/dutch-data-watchdog-highlights-safety-issues-with-ai-glasses/5311345) highlights another part of the regulator’s findings: recording indicators could be difficult to see and easy to conceal, and some devices had weaknesses that allowed unauthorized access to recordings or camera control. A person nearby cannot make an informed choice if they cannot tell what is being captured.

Our view: a workplace should decide its recording rules before introducing the device. Explain where recording is permitted, who can access the resulting material, and how a colleague can object. An accessibility need deserves a workable accommodation, not an assumption that continuous recording is necessary for every task.

The regulator acknowledges that these tools can help people understand their surroundings. That makes reliability and privacy more important. Useful assistance must come with evidence that it works, clear limits on what it can do, and accountability when it fails.
