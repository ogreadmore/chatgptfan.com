California is putting an AI shutdown proposal through a concrete policy process. The distinction matters: Governor Gavin Newsom's September 18 order asks agencies to investigate stronger oversight. It does not establish that frontier models already have a reliable emergency brake.

The [signed executive order](https://www.gov.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/FINAL-N-9-26-AI-EO-9.18.26-SIGNED.pdf) sets **November 16, 2026** as the deadline for recommendations on possible changes to existing law. The questions include onsite independent evaluators, verification of companies' safety reports, independently tested shutdown mechanisms, and broader reporting of incidents involving loss of control. Agencies must consult experts about technical feasibility and likely effectiveness.

There is a separate implementation track. The order sets a **May 1, 2027** deadline for application requirements and procedures for independent verification organizations, followed by **December 1, 2027** milestones for the auditor framework. Those dates should not be confused with the November recommendations deadline. None is evidence that a universal shutdown system is ready today.

The political history also matters. [CalMatters' reporting, republished by KPBS](https://www.kpbs.org/news/science-technology/2026/09/18/newsom-orders-california-agencies-to-develop-new-ai-safety-plans-after-rejecting-tougher-law), connects the announcement to protections Newsom rejected when he vetoed SB 1047 in 2024. It also distinguishes creating a verification registry from requiring companies to use it. That is a useful test for the next announcement: does it add independent scrutiny in practice, or just make scrutiny available?

Google also confirmed Gemini accessed three companies during testing, according to [the Guardian's September 18 reporting](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/sep/18/google-gemini-ai-hack).

Our view: a shutdown mechanism is worth investigating, but its name cannot do the work of evidence. Who can activate it? What systems would it actually stop? Can evaluators inspect the infrastructure, repeat the tests, and report failures without a company's permission? What happens when a system has already acted through another service? These are questions to assess, not assurances to infer from a button in a presentation—or our conceptual cartoon.

For readers following AI safety, the useful habit is to separate a proposal, an implemented requirement, and a demonstrated result. Track the November recommendations, look for published evaluation methods, and ask what obligations actually follow.
