Governments have agreed to continue talks on autonomous weapons with the aim of developing rules by 2028. They have not agreed that those rules must be legally binding. That distinction is the central result of the Geneva negotiations that ended early on November 21, according to [Reuters’ reporting from the conference](https://www.arabnews.com/world/states-agree-to-un-talks-aiming-to-regulate-killer-robots-by-2028-3013276).

Reuters reports that around 100 countries supported a stronger, binding approach, while major arms-producing states including the United States, Russia and Israel opposed it. The resulting agreement preserves a route toward further rules, but leaves their legal force unresolved. A timetable for negotiations is not a new prohibition on deployment.

The [UN’s conference page](https://meetings.unoda.org/ccw-revcon/convention-on-certain-conventional-weapons-seventh-review-conference-2026) provides the official documents. At our review, it still listed draft final texts. We are attributing the overnight outcome to Reuters rather than presenting a draft as the adopted agreement.

## What needs human control?

The issue is a weapon’s ability to select a target and apply force without a person making that specific decision. The [International Committee of the Red Cross explains](https://www.icrc.org/en/article/faq-autonomous-weapon-systems) that an armed drone is not necessarily autonomous: a human may remotely choose its targets and decide when to fire. Autonomous flight alone does not answer the question, and not every autonomous weapon uses AI.

Existing international humanitarian law already applies. The ICRC nevertheless [calls for additional binding rules](https://www.icrc.org/en/law-and-policy/autonomous-weapons): prohibit unpredictable autonomous weapons and those designed or used to apply force against people, and tightly restrict the rest. Those are the organization’s recommendations, not rules newly adopted this week.

## A promise is only the beginning

There is a separate diplomatic development. On November 20, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Donald Trump and Xi Jinping had agreed humans should remain involved in military decisions using AI. [Reuters reported his account of the meeting](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/trump-xi-agreed-that-military-ai-decisions-should-involve-humans-bessent-says-5042288). That statement does not establish a detailed international enforcement system or resolve the Geneva disagreement.

Our view: meaningful control needs more than a person nominally appearing somewhere in the process. The practical questions are whether that person understands the proposed use of force, has enough time to judge it, can stop it, and can be held accountable. Negotiators should be pressed for specific protections, enforceable obligations and transparent progress.

For readers, the useful next step is to follow the final mandate and the rules it produces. Look for what governments actually commit to—and how those commitments protect people—rather than treating another meeting or a reassuring phrase as the safeguard itself.
