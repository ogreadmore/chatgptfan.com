President Trump said on September 19 that he plans to create an “AI Force” and appoint an AI czar. [Reuters reported](https://currently.att.yahoo.com/att/trump-says-create-ai-force-172503098.html) that he paired the announcement with a commitment to support AI growth and rely on existing civil and criminal justice systems to address wrongdoing. It is a new announcement in the argument over the pace of AI, rather than a reason to assume that argument has been settled.

**The practical details are still missing.** [Axios reports](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/19/trump-ai-czar-space-force-safety) that the proposed body's duties, budget and place in government remain unspecified. For readers, the next useful document will be one that explains what this organization can actually do.

The competing industry proposal is more specific about access. In [his September essay](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier), Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei calls for outside evaluators embedded at frontier AI companies, followed by coordination among companies in democracies and then internationally. He says Anthropic is committing to the evaluator step itself. His definition of pacing allows continued training and technical progress while making more time for safeguards and external assessment.

That is a company leader's proposal, with commercial interests attached. Its value should be judged through implementation: who gets access, what they can inspect, and what they can tell the public. Amodei proposes publication rights for important findings, subject to specified redactions, and says unfavorable conclusions alone should not be grounds for withholding them. Those are commitments worth tracking, rather than treating the announcement as proof that independent scrutiny is already working.

Our view: enthusiasm for useful AI and demands for accountable development belong together. The question for a new government office is whether it helps the public understand and influence consequential decisions. A name and a senior appointment cannot answer that question by themselves. Equally, a reassuring statement from a lab needs evidence behind it.

For the next round of announcements, we will look for three things: a published mandate, a clear route for reporting problems, and an explanation of who can require a response. We will also distinguish future proposals from measures already operating. That keeps attention on consequences without asking readers to accept either promises of safety or predictions of catastrophe on authority alone.
