Australia has moved from negotiating an online-safety deal to passing it. On November 25, Parliament approved the Digital Duty of Care legislation, covering services including social platforms, games, apps and AI chatbots. The government's announcement says the duty will take effect in 12 months. Passage is a significant development; it does not mean the new protections are operating today.

The central shift is responsibility for product design. According to the communications minister's announcement, providers must protect Australians from seriously harmful material and conduct, and address harm from their own features. The package includes additional protections for under-18s, user control over social-media algorithms, and a route for individuals or groups to sue over breaches that cause loss or damage. The government describes maximum penalties as 5% of global turnover or A$109.2 million, whichever is greater. [Read the announcement](https://minister.infrastructure.gov.au/wells/media-release/australias-digital-duty-care-becomes-law).

That makes the question bigger than whether a chatbot will refuse one dangerous prompt. Our reading is that accountability also needs to reach the incentives and design choices around the conversation: what a service encourages, how it responds when something goes wrong, and whether an affected person has a usable remedy.

## Passage is a beginning

[ABC's parliamentary coverage](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-25/federal-politics-live-blog-parliament-november-25/107422208) records the final passage after Senate amendments returned to the House. It also reports rule-making powers for a complaints ombudsman. Those powers should not be mistaken for a fully functioning complaints service already available to readers.

The legislation remains contested. [Euronews and AP report](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/25/australia-follows-eu-with-new-online-safety-law-putting-big-tech-at-risk-of-multibillion-d) that opposition leader Angus Taylor argues it threatens free speech and says he would repeal it. That is the opposition's position, rather than an established finding about how the law will operate.

**Our view:** platforms should have enforceable responsibilities for harms they can foresee and prevent. Public regulators also need clear limits, transparency and meaningful ways to challenge their decisions. These are complementary demands. A safety label alone cannot demonstrate that either side has met them.

During implementation, watch for the actual rules, the evidence providers must supply, and the accessibility of redress. An announced right is less useful if exercising it is prohibitively difficult. We will track those details separately from the political celebration of passage.

This development concerns online services; it is not the superintelligence-development prohibition supported by this publication.
