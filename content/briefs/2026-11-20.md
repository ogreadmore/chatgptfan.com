The AI boom has a physical footprint: power stations, grid connections and cooling equipment behind the chat window. The International Energy Agency’s [World Energy Outlook 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-outlook-2026), released November 19, projects global data-centre electricity consumption doubling to around 1,000 terawatt-hours in 2030 under its Stated Policies Scenario. AI-optimised facilities account for roughly three-quarters of that growth.

These are scenario results, not a measurement of what has already happened—or a forecast of the footprint of your next ChatGPT question. The report covers conventional computing as well as AI.

## Renewable contracts do not tell the whole story

The [original report’s AI section, pages 82–86](https://www.iea.org/files/41082112-1153-4b16-b738-d44fb273dfd9/WorldEnergyOutlook2026.pdf#page=82), distinguishes renewable-energy purchasing agreements from the physical supply reaching a site. Nearly half of commercial data-centre demand is backed by renewable agreements, yet coal and gas currently provide around 60% of the sector’s physical electricity supply.

As fossil-fuel generation expands alongside renewables, the IEA projects a 70% rise in associated emissions by 2030. Its regional outlook differs sharply: the US leans heavily on gas, China on coal, and the EU on renewables. Announced infrastructure is not completed infrastructure: most planned US gas capacity linked to data centres is not expected to operate until the 2030s.

That distinction matters when judging a company’s environmental promises. Buying renewable electricity and ensuring that a facility has low-emissions power when it operates are related questions, but they are not interchangeable evidence.

## The local bill needs local evidence

[Euronews’ reporting](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/19/data-centre-energy-consumption-could-double-by-2030-straining-grids-iea-warns) highlights constrained grids and equipment supplies, alongside opposition over land, water and infrastructure. Those pressures reach people who may never use an AI service.

The IEA also cautions that analysis does not yet establish a clear relationship between data-centre capacity growth and electricity-price increases. Costs can shift to other customers when shared investment charges rise; better use of existing capacity can have the opposite effect. Local tariffs, available capacity and cost allocation matter.

## What to ask before accepting the promise

Our view: enthusiasm for useful AI should come with scrutiny of the infrastructure supporting it. For a proposed project or a vendor’s sustainability claim, ask:

- What electricity will physically supply the facility, including when renewable output is low?
- Who pays for new connections, generation and network upgrades?
- What public evidence will show whether the promised emissions and community protections were delivered?

For everyday users, this report does not supply a universal emissions number per prompt. For businesses and policymakers, it does supply a reason to demand clearer evidence before treating rapid expansion as an unqualified public benefit. Better tools are worth pursuing; their costs should be visible too.
