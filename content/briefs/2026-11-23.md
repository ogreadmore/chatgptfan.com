OpenAI’s chief scientist wants frontier laboratories to agree on limits to further development. That is a consequential proposal. It is also different from having an agreed limit, an independent test, and a working mechanism for stopping.

In a [November 22 essay](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/), Jakub Pachocki proposes using perplexity, alongside other measurements, to help set common limits. It measures how well a model predicts unseen tokens; lower values mean better predictions. Progress beyond a limit would depend on a concrete improvement in understanding and monitoring model behavior. [Reuters reports](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/openai-scientist-proposes-industry-ai-limits-tied-to-model-intelligence-metric-5044174) that Sam Altman endorsed the proposal.

### What the number cannot tell us

The essay acknowledges important limitations: laboratories use different evaluation data, improvements outside pretraining can increase capabilities, and the score does not directly measure a real-world task. OpenAI’s published measurements use its own internal codebase. Treat them as company-reported research, not an independently established safety threshold.

The proposal does not provide a signed industry agreement or a ready-to-enforce numerical ceiling. Nor does a lower prediction error establish that a system is safe.

### What would make a limit meaningful?

Our view: the next useful announcement would answer four practical questions.

- **Who measures it?** Independent evaluators need enough access to reproduce relevant tests and challenge a developer’s account. Comparing numbers requires knowing what was measured.
- **What triggers a stop?** A commitment needs a defined threshold, the activities it covers, and a process for handling uncertainty before the next training run or deployment.
- **Who can enforce it?** The public needs to know who can require a pause, inspect compliance, and respond when commercial incentives push in the other direction.
- **What permits restarting?** The evidence required to proceed should be specified in advance, with responsibility for the decision clearly assigned.

These are editorial criteria for judging a future arrangement, not claims that this proposal already satisfies them. A useful research metric can inform oversight without becoming its substitute.

For readers, the distinction matters: welcoming a serious discussion about limits does not require treating reassurance as implementation. We will look for published commitments, independently examined evidence and accountable decisions.
