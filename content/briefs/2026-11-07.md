An employee gets a confidential request from the CEO. On a call, the voice sounds familiar. The CEO directs the employee to a lawyer at a recognizable firm, and the lawyer confirms where to send the money. That second conversation sounds like a sensible precaution. In an indictment unsealed on November 5 in New York, prosecutors allege that the same group supplied both voices. [U.S. Attorney’s account](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/three-foreign-nationals-arrested-and-charged-scheme-use-super-intelligence-defraud)

Federal prosecutors charged three people in a scheme they say took about $129 million from organizations including two hospital systems, a bank and a pet food company. According to the indictment, the group impersonated executives on calls using AI to mimic their voices and, in some cases, their faces. One participant allegedly kept a handwritten script for the lawyer’s part, including lines meant to keep employees from discussing the request with colleagues. The defendants are presumed innocent. ## Who chose the second number?

A call can answer two different questions. Does this person sound like my boss? Did my boss actually authorize this payment? In the prosecutors’ account, the purported CEO chose whom the employee should call next. Even a careful employee who asked the lawyer to repeat the amount or explain the transaction would still be checking with someone the first caller had selected.

The FBI recommends ending a suspicious call and reaching the person or organization through contact details you find independently. At work, that could mean opening the company directory you already use and calling the executive’s office. If an outside law firm is involved, its published switchboard can connect you to the named attorney. Ask about the specific payment and destination before authorizing it. The same check works when the audio is clear and the caller looks exactly right. [FBI guidance on AI impersonation](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)

If a transfer has already gone out, the FBI advises contacting the bank that sent it immediately to request a recall or reversal, then filing a complaint with its Internet Crime Complaint Center. Have the transaction details ready. [IC3 response instructions](https://www.ic3.gov/CrimeInfo/BEC)

If you handle invoices, try this with a colleague before the next urgent request. Name the person who can confirm an unusual payment, find their number in your own records, and make sure both of you can reach them.
