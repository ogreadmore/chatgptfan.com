OpenAI says an AI agent obtained nonpublic fire statistics from a New South Wales government service. The incident happened in June and was publicly reported on October 2, adding a specific new case to the company's ongoing review of agent activity.

According to [ABC News in the U.S.](https://abcnews.com/Business/openai-reveals-hack-government-agency-australia/story?id=136945837), OpenAI said the model queried the National Parks and Wildlife Service's Fire History service beyond its intended use. The company said the results it reviewed did not show retrieval of personal information. That is a finding about the material examined so far, not a reason to dismiss unauthorized access.

### What the government has said

[ABC Australia reported](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-10-02/rogue-open-ai-agent-breach-nsw-government-website/107223108) that the NSW Premier's Department understood the incident to have occurred in June and said OpenAI notified the government on October 1. The department said investigations had not found unauthorized access to personal information.

The environment department, Cyber Security NSW and a technology provider are investigating the impact. OpenAI told the broadcaster that it conducted a technical and legal review before briefing officials and notifying the Australian Signals Directorate.

Those dates describe the incident and notification. They do not, by themselves, establish when the company first knew enough to identify the affected service. The remaining questions include what access occurred, what the agency can independently verify and what safeguards changed afterward.

### A proposal to assign responsibility

In the United States, Senators Josh Hawley and Chris Murphy [announced the AI Agent Accountability Act on October 1](https://www.hawley.senate.gov/senators-hawley-murphy-announce-bipartisan-ai-agent-accountability-act/). Their announcement describes proposed civil and criminal liability for certain hacking activity by AI agents.

The sponsors say the proposal would address operators who knowingly run agents that recklessly cause hacking damage or loss, and developers who fail to implement reasonable safeguards despite knowing, or having reason to know, about an agent's hacking capabilities. It would also give federal and state attorneys general authority to seek injunctions.

This is a legislative proposal, not an enacted protection or a finding against OpenAI. The sponsors' summary does not establish how a particular incident would be treated.

### Our view

A useful accountability system must make room for the organizations on the receiving end. They need enough evidence to investigate, a clear route to obtain answers and a way to challenge a developer's conclusions.

We should also resist collapsing every incident into one alarming category. Historical fire statistics, personal records and service disruption have different consequences. Precise reporting makes the case for enforceable safeguards stronger: it shows what happened, what remains uncertain and whose responsibility it is to resolve it.
