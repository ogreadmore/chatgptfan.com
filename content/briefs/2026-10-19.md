The World Bank is making a case for AI built around everyday needs in developing economies. The Guardian [reported the push on October 18](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/18/world-bank-artificial-intelligence-developing-economies). A [World Bank summit scheduled for October 19–22 in Seoul](https://live.worldbank.org/en/event/2026/global-ai-digital-summit-small-ai) puts locally adapted tools on the agenda; its programme is not evidence that their promised benefits have arrived.

The Bank's [small-AI explainer](https://www.worldbank.org/en/brief/2026/04/02/small-ai-big-impact) emphasizes affordable applications for specific problems, accessible through ordinary devices where infrastructure and skills are limited. Here, “small” describes a practical approach to access and use. It should not be read as a guarantee that every application runs offline, avoids cloud services or uses a small language model.

## A useful example, with limits

In an [earlier farming feature](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/feature/2026/08/27/small-ai-transforms-farming-in-india), the Bank describes Indian farmers using phone photographs and local-language questions to seek advice about crops. That is a concrete task with a clear user need.

The same feature describes Kerala's KATHIR platform as being developed with Bank support. Some capabilities, including advice from uploaded crop photographs, are described in terms of a full rollout. We should not turn those plans into a claim that every feature is already available. This is the funder's account of projects it supports, not an independent evaluation of their accuracy or farmers' outcomes.

## Adoption still needs scrutiny

The [World Development Report 2026 webpage](https://www.worldbank.org/en/publication/wdr2026) argues for adapting tools to local languages, data and institutions, rather than assuming that importing a model is sufficient. It also identifies dependence on a few suppliers and shortages of reliable infrastructure and skills as constraints. Its chapter summaries acknowledge that benefits are not guaranteed and that evidence for some public-service uses remains limited.

We reviewed that overview and its main messages, not the full report. Its policy recommendations are the Bank's position; including the resource does not mean adopting every recommendation.

For readers building with ChatGPT or another AI tool, our practical takeaway is to begin with a user and a task. What device do they actually have? Can they understand the answer in their own language? What happens when connectivity fails or advice is wrong?

Test those conditions with intended users, compare cost per useful result, and provide a route to a qualified person when an error could cause harm. Ask who controls uploaded information and whether people can obtain the service without surrendering unnecessary data.

Affordable access is worth pursuing. It still needs evidence, meaningful safeguards and public accountability. A useful application does not settle the separate question of whether increasingly powerful AI is being developed safely.
